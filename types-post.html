A nickname is a name people use instead of, or alongside, someone’s real name. The word is older than it looks: it comes from the Middle English “an ekename”, meaning an added name, which people misheard over time as “a nekename”.

Most nicknames fall into a handful of types. Knowing them makes it easier to pick one that suits the person, or to make your own from scratch. Below are the eight main types of nicknames, with examples, who each one fits, and a simple way to make a nickname from any name.

The 8 types of nicknames at a glance

1. Shortened names

This is the most common type. You keep one part of the name and drop the rest. Usually it is the start (Alex from Alexander, Sam from Samantha), but it can be the end (Xander from Alexander, Beth from Elizabeth) or the middle (Liz from Elizabeth).

Shortened names feel neutral, so they work almost anywhere: at school, at work and in the family. Many of them, like Alex and Beth, are now given as full names in their own right. To find one, say the full name slowly and listen for the part that stands out.

2. Pet forms

A pet form adds a soft ending to a name or its short form, most often -y or -ie: Johnny, Katie, Sammy, Danny. Language experts call these diminutives, because the ending makes the name sound smaller and warmer.

Parents use pet forms for children, and friends use them in a playful way. Some people outgrow them, though, so a grown-up Johnny may prefer John at work. If you are not sure, ask which version they like.

3. Rhyming and sound-swap nicknames

Some of the best-known English nicknames started as rhymes. Will became Bill, and Meg became Peg and then Peggy. According to Mental Floss, rhyming nicknames like these were common in the Middle Ages, which is why some short forms look nothing like the names they came from.

Today rhymes are mostly for fun: Dan the Man, Kate the Great, Al the Pal. They fit best among friends and on teams, where a bit of silliness is welcome.

4. Initials

An initials nickname uses the first letters of a person’s names, usually the first and middle or the first and last: JT, AJ, CJ. It is popular when two people in the same group share a first name, or when a full name is long.

Initials are short, easy to call out and work for anyone. Before you settle on one, check that the letters do not spell or sound like something awkward.

5. Surname nicknames

On sports teams, in the military and in many offices, people go by a form of their last name. Smith becomes Smitty, Jones becomes Jonesy, and names that start with Mac or Mc often become Mac.

A surname nickname sounds friendly but still a little formal, so it suits teammates and coworkers. It also helps when a group has several people with the same first name.

6. Alliterative nicknames

An alliterative nickname puts a word in front of the name that starts with the same sound: Mighty Mike, Sunny Sam, Jazzy Jen. The repeated sound makes the name easy to remember, which is why so many cartoon characters and stage names use it.

These work for friends, children and teammates, and they make good online names too. Pick a word that matches the person, not just the letter.

7. Descriptive and ironic nicknames

A descriptive nickname points at a trait, a habit or a role: Speedy for a fast runner, Doc for the friend who always has a bandage, Red for someone with red hair. An ironic nickname flips the idea, like Tiny for the tallest person in the room.

These are the nicknames most likely to stick for life, so keep them kind. A nickname about someone’s looks or a mistake they made can hurt, even if it started as a joke.

8. Terms of endearment

A term of endearment, often called a pet name, replaces the name with a word of affection: Honey, Sweetie, Babe, Bean, Bug. Most are about food, small animals or sweetness.

How to make a nickname from a name

You can make a good nickname from almost any name in a few minutes. Here is the method, using Christopher James Walker as the example:

  1. Write out the full name. First, middle and last: Christopher James Walker.
  2. Pull out the short forms. Take the start, the end and any strong middle part: Chris, Topher, and Kit, a traditional short form of Christopher.
  3. Add a soft ending. Try -y or -ie on the short forms: Chrissy.
  4. Try initials and the surname. CJ from the first two names, or Walker on its own.
  5. Play with sound. Add a rhyme or an alliteration: Chris the Wiz, Cool Chris.
  6. Say each one out loud. Picture calling the person by it, and keep the two or three that feel right. Better still, let them choose.

Tips for a nickname people actually like

  • Ask first, or at least watch how they react the first few times.
  • Keep it kind: no jokes about looks, weight or mistakes.
  • Match the setting: terms of endearment do not belong at work.
  • Keep it easy to say and spell.
  • Let it grow naturally: the nicknames that last usually come from a shared moment.
