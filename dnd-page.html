What makes a good D&D name

A good D&D name tells the table something about your character before you say a word. A dwarf called Thrumik Sootmine sounds like someone who grew up near a forge, while a halfling called Polella Thistlefoot sounds like someone who would rather be at home with a cup of tea. The race sets the sound, and the family name hints at where the character comes from.

It also has to work at the table. You will hear the name every session, so it should be easy to say out loud and easy for the other players to remember. That is why every result here shows a pronunciation: if you can say it once without stumbling, it will hold up for a whole campaign.

D&D names by race

Each race in D&D has its own naming style. Here is what to expect from each one, with a link to our dedicated generator where we have one.

Human

Humans are the most varied race in D&D, so their names can be almost anything. Most follow a simple pattern: a short given name and a family name tied to a place, a trade or a landmark, like Edan Hartford or Merise Millbrook. Our human names are built from short, solid syllables so they sound at home in a fantasy world without feeling borrowed from one culture. For more options, try the character name generator.

Elf

Elf names are long and flowing, full of soft vowels and l, r and th sounds. In common D&D lore, elves use a childhood name until they come of age and then choose an adult name, so an elf’s name often feels chosen rather than given. Family names tend to describe nature, like Brooksong or Lightcrown. For more options, try the elf name generator.

Half-elf

Half-elves usually take a name from whichever side raised them. A half-elf who grew up among elves might have a flowing elven name, while one raised in a human town might have a plain human one. Our half-elf names lean elven but stay shorter and easier to say. For more options, try the elf name generator.

Drow

Drow names are sharp and dark, with y, x and z sounds. In drow society, the noble house matters more than the person, so a house name carries weight. A drow who left the Underdark often takes a new surname that tells their story, like Sunblind. For more options, try the drow name generator.

Dwarf

Dwarf names are short and hard, full of consonants like k, g and r. The clan name is a matter of pride, and many clan names come from mining and smithing: soot, stone, anvils and barrels. For more options, try the dwarf name generator.

Halfling

Halfling names are friendly and easy, with soft endings like -ie, -o and -ly. Family names usually come from everyday comforts: food, gardens, homes and drink. Halflings also love nicknames, so do not be surprised if your Pipwick gets called Pip by the whole party.

Gnome

Gnomes are known for collecting names: one from their parents, a few from friends and a nickname or two. Their names are quick and playful, with bright sounds like z, b and i. Family names often point to tinkering, like Cogwick or Pebblebolt.

Half-orc

Half-orc names usually depend on where the character grew up. Those raised among orcs carry short, harsh names like Grugar or Bashul, while others take human names. Our half-orc family names are fierce epithets, like Stonejaw or Wolfhide, that sound earned in battle.

Dragonborn

Dragonborn names are rough and powerful, with growling r and sh sounds. The clan is central to a dragonborn’s identity, and at many tables the clan name is said first as a mark of honor, so feel free to flip the order of a name you like. For more options, try the dragonborn name generator.

Tiefling

Tieflings often carry infernal names with a hard, ancient sound, like Rynak. Others choose a virtue name, such as Candor or Hope, that describes who they want to be. Some use both: an infernal name with a virtue name as a nickname. For more options, try the tiefling name generator.

Tips for naming your D&D character

  • Say it out loud. If you trip over it once, you will trip over it every session.
  • Make it easy to tell apart. Avoid a name that starts with the same sound as another player’s character.
  • Let the family name tell a story. A clan name like Sootmine or an exile name like Sunblind does half the backstory for you.
  • Keep a short form. Long elf and dragonborn names are great, but your party will shorten them anyway, so pick a nickname you like.
  • Ask your DM about the setting. Some campaigns have their own naming customs, and a name that fits the world makes the story stronger.
